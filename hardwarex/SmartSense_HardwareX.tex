SmartSense Monitoring: an open-source wireless PT100 node and gateway for surface-temperature monitoring of industrial equipment

\textbf{Authors}\\

\textbf{Abstract}\\
SmartSense Monitoring is an open-source wireless PT100 node and gateway for industrial surface-temperature monitoring. Each battery-powered node combines a XIAO ESP32-C6, a three-wire PT100 probe, a MAX31865 front-end and an Ebyte E220 LoRa radio at 433~MHz. The gateway stores a rolling 30-day history locally and serves a Wi-Fi dashboard without a cloud service. A three-node evaluation includes a 32-day pilot deployment and an extended Node B record, totalling 51{,}338 samples across the individual recording windows. Raw availability was 76.0\%, 32.3\% and 75.1\%; link availability conditioned on inferred gateway-active periods was 97.8--98.4\%. Arrival intervals support an inferred PDR of 95.3--96.6\%. Five paired temperature readings provide field agreement after offset adjustment, without establishing absolute accuracy. A five-day partial discharge supports estimated autonomy of 177 days by linear extrapolation and 291--307 days using a LiPo-curve model, not demonstrated service lifetime. A temperature rise was later associated with a bearing anomaly during inspection, supporting condition monitoring without validated diagnosis or prediction. Hardware, firmware, software and analysis artifacts are versioned in a public repository; absent primary CSV files and incomplete component identification limit full replication of the evaluation.

\textbf{Keywords}\\
Open-source hardware; LoRa; PT100; RTD; MAX31865; ESP32-C6; industrial temperature monitoring; condition monitoring

\section{Hardware in context}

Continuous temperature monitoring of motors and other industrial equipment helps identify conditions associated with friction, overload, poor ventilation or mechanical degradation. In a manual inspection, an operator obtains only the value at the instant of measurement; if that value is not systematically recorded, the thermal evolution of the equipment cannot be reconstructed or related to its operating and stop cycles.

Wireless sensor networks distribute measurement points without installing communication cabling at each location [1,2,6]. For low-data-rate applications, LoRa provides a long-range, low-power alternative suited to battery-powered nodes, whose range and scalability have been characterised experimentally [3,7,8]. Relevant precedents include LoRaWAN temperature monitoring with a local-server option [9], an open condition-monitoring sensor with vibration and acoustic measurements [10], and the local SD-based HIGROTERM logger [12]. SmartSense contributes the integration of an external three-wire PT100 and MAX31865, point-to-point radio, battery operation and a gateway-hosted daily history and dashboard. Its contribution is this documented platform and pilot evaluation, rather than invention of its constituent technologies or superiority over all existing systems. It supports condition-based maintenance [4,11] but does not implement or validate a predictive-maintenance algorithm.

A PT100 RTD was selected for its established industrial use, three-wire lead compensation with the MAX31865 and availability in a magnetic surface probe suitable for motor housings. The installed probe is identified only as a magnetic three-wire PT100 in the bill of materials: manufacturer, exact reference, tolerance class and rated assembly range are not documented. The generic RTD conversion range therefore cannot be assigned to the complete installed probe, cable or enclosure. The field comparison below characterises agreement in a narrow temperature interval, not the full measurement range or inter-node repeatability.

Compared with proprietary industrial loggers such as the Advantech WISE-2410, SmartSense Monitoring trades integrated vibration sensing for full openness and local-first operation. Table~\ref{tab:comparison} contrasts both systems by capability rather than price alone, making it suitable for pilot plants and educational settings where flexibility and reproducibility matter more than a turnkey platform.

\section{Hardware description}

SmartSense Monitoring consists of one or more battery-powered sensor nodes and a gateway/visualizer. Each node acquires the surface temperature of a machine with a magnetic PT100 probe and a MAX31865 RTD front-end, estimates its battery state, and transmits the reading over a 433~MHz LoRa link before entering deep sleep. The gateway receives the frames, identifies each node by its MAC address, applies a configurable offset, stores the samples locally and presents them on an integrated touch display and a Wi-Fi web dashboard.

The documented radio configuration uses 9600~bit/s UART (8N1), 2.4~kbit/s air rate, channel 23 in the stated 433~MHz band and fixed addressing. These settings are available in the project connection guide and firmware; the exact radio variant and final pilot transmit power are not verified. A 120~s nominal cycle provides 720 expected samples per node per day, balancing thermal-history resolution with traffic, storage and battery demand for this pilot.

\begin{itemize}
\item[$\bullet$] It provides automatic periodic surface-temperature logging using commercially available modules and an itemized bill of materials.
\item[$\bullet$] It operates fully offline: the gateway serves its own dashboard over a local Wi-Fi access point, so no cloud account or Internet connection is required.
\item[$\bullet$] It preserves a per-sensor daily history, enabling the study of thermal trends, transients and machine on/off cycles that a spot measurement cannot capture.
\item[$\bullet$] It is a modular basis for further work: additional nodes, alternative RTD probes, or extra variables (e.g. current, vibration) can be integrated by reusing the node design.
\item[$\bullet$] Published project schematics and source code support teaching wireless sensor networks, RTD acquisition and local data handling; the purchased modules retain their manufacturer-specific internals.
\end{itemize}

\section{Build instructions}

The following steps describe the construction of one sensor node and the gateway. Refer to the node and gateway schematics (Design files) and to the Bill of materials for component designators.

\textbf{Sensor node}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
\item Use a three-wire magnetic PT100 probe and document its manufacturer, reference, tolerance and rated assembly temperature before replication; these fields are missing for the pilot probe. Configure the MAX31865 module in three-wire mode (bridge the 2/3-wire jumper), and connect the PT100 leads: the two leads from the same probe end to F+ and RTD+, and the remaining lead to RTD-, bridged to F-.
\item Wire the MAX31865 SPI bus to the XIAO ESP32-C6: CLK\,$\rightarrow$\,D6 (GPIO16), SDO\,$\rightarrow$\,D5 (GPIO23), SDI\,$\rightarrow$\,D4 (GPIO22), CS\,$\rightarrow$\,D3 (GPIO21). Power the module VIN from D2 (GPIO2) so the firmware can switch it off during deep sleep.
\item Connect the E220 LoRa module by UART: E220 TXD\,$\rightarrow$\,D7 (GPIO17), RXD\,$\rightarrow$\,D8 (GPIO19), M0\,$\rightarrow$\,D10 (GPIO18), M1\,$\rightarrow$\,D9 (GPIO20); power VCC/GND from 3V3/GND.
\item Build the battery divider: VBAT\,--\,R1 (344.8~k$\Omega$)\,--\,A0 (GPIO0)\,--\,R2 (994.3~k$\Omega$)\,--\,GND. Connect the LiPo cell through the TP4056 charge/protection module to the XIAO battery pads.
\item Flash the node firmware (\texttt{Nodo\_Lora.ino}) with the Arduino IDE and the ESP32 board package. Install the Adafruit\_MAX31865 library. Set \texttt{RREF} to the measured reference resistance of your MAX31865 module (421.1~$\Omega$ in this build).
\item Fit the components in the 3D-printed PLA enclosure and attach the PT100 magnetically to the target surface.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Gateway}
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
\item Connect the E220 module to the ESP32-C6 Touch LCD board: E220 TXD\,$\rightarrow$\,GPIO8 (gateway RX), RXD\,$\rightarrow$\,GPIO7 (gateway TX), and both M0 and M1 to GPIO9; power VCC/GND from 3V3/GND.
\item Optionally wire a critical-alarm output (LED, buzzer or relay driver) to GPIO16.
\item Flash the gateway firmware (\texttt{maestro\_Lora\_pantalla.ino}) and upload the web dashboard assets to the on-board storage (LittleFS). Insert a microSD card for the history.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Safety.} Use a fused LiPo cell with the TP4056 protection module and observe LiPo handling precautions. Ensure the PT100 and enclosure are rated for the surface temperature of the monitored equipment and mounted so they do not interfere with moving parts.

\section{Operation instructions}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
\item Power the gateway. It creates a Wi-Fi access point (\texttt{Sensores\_Temp}) and serves the dashboard at \texttt{http://192.168.4.1}.
\item Power each node. On first boot it transmits periodically (default configurable between 5 and 7200~s; 120~s in the pilot). The gateway registers each node by its MAC address.
\item In the dashboard, assign names to sensors, group them by machine, set the sampling interval, apply a per-node temperature offset, and configure warning and critical thresholds.
\item Monitor the live values (temperature, battery, connectivity) and consult the per-day 24-hour history, including minimum, maximum and average, and compare sensors of the same machine.
\item Preserve copies of the per-sensor stored history before the 30-day rotation removes older dates. The project analysed exported CSVs and the software replica can import them; direct web CSV download is not verified in the reviewed gateway firmware and is not claimed as a demonstrated interface function.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Validation and characterization}

The three-node evaluation includes a 32-day pilot deployment (8 May--9 June 2026 for Nodes A and C), with Node B recorded from 10 May to 20 July 2026. The 51{,}338 samples are the sum of these individual files, not a count over 32 complete days common to all three nodes. Figures comparing nodes use their overlapping interval. The nominal cycle was 120~s and the documented median inter-arrival interval was 121~s. Separate partial-discharge and radio-range tests were performed in August.

\textbf{Availability.} Table~\ref{tab:avail} separates raw system-level data availability from link availability conditioned on inferred gateway-active periods. The generator \texttt{analisis/figuras\_validacion.py} uses 121~s as the expected cadence. Raw availability divides received samples by the expected count across each node's first-to-last-record span. Conditioned availability removes the entire duration of each inter-arrival gap exceeding 363~s before computing the expected count. Simultaneous gaps in Fig.~\ref{fig:cobertura} support a common-cause interpretation, including gateway power interruptions during plant stoppages; they do not independently prove the power state or distinguish reception, clock and storage failures. Thus 97.8--98.4\% describes classified active periods, not overall system availability, and the excluded hours cannot all be asserted to be confirmed power-off hours.

\textbf{Reception cadence and inferred packet delivery.} Retained short intervals cluster near a 121~s median (Fig.~\ref{fig:intervalos}). The separate generator \texttt{analisis/perdida\_paquetes.py} uses a 120~s nominal cycle and excludes intervals longer than 1800~s. For retained gaps it rounds the interval divided by 120~s to $k$, infers $k-1$ missing frames when $k\geq2$, and computes received/(received + inferred missing). The documented inferred PDR is 96.55\%, 96.07\% and 95.32\% for A, B and C, respectively: 95.3--96.6\% rounded, with inferred loss of 3.5--4.7\%. Each estimate uses its individual node file, including Node B's extension. These filters and denominators differ from availability; neither metric is a direct sent-versus-received count. Without a historical sequence counter and send log, cadence assumptions cannot distinguish radio loss from absent acquisition or persistence.

\textbf{Representative 60-minute window.} The historical window of 10 May 2026, 00:00--01:00 (Fig.~\ref{fig:prueba60}), contains 29, 30 and 30 records for A, B and C against 30 nominally expected at a 120~s cycle: received/expected ratios of 96.7\%, 100\% and 100\%. The denominator is a schedule, not an observed transmission count. The script selects a window with at least 25 records per node, so this is a coverage-selected illustration, not an independent test or an unbiased estimate for all hours. RSSI was enabled later for the range test; the interface used did not expose SNR. A sequence counter added after the deployment supports future direct PDR testing but cannot recover historical sends. End-to-end latency and service recovery require synchronized node/gateway measurements.

\textbf{Range test.} On 20 August 2026 a node moved away from the gateway and back in an urban non-line-of-sight path with buildings in between (Fig.~\ref{fig:distancia}). Valid packets were received at the farthest observed distance, approximately 688~m, with RSSI near $-98$~dBm at 20:44--20:54, compared with approximately $-15$~dBm nearby. The test ended voluntarily with the link still operational: at least 688~m is a demonstrated lower bound in this geometry. No numerical sensitivity margin is claimed because the exact E220 variant and final physical configuration are incompletely documented. The recorded battery indicator stayed near 94\% during 2.6~h; this voltage-derived indication does not measure charge consumption. Motion and changing obstacles prevent a controlled RSSI--distance characterization; fixed positions and repeated trials remain future work.

The FLIR image (Fig.~\ref{fig:termica}), attributed in the field record to a C8 camera, corroborates the location and approximate 70--71~$^{\circ}$C magnitude of the hotspot; it is not documented as synchronized to every paired reading. The result is field consistency, not traceable calibration. The Fluke was also used to adjust the offset, so the comparison is not independent. The project cites instrument specifications of $\pm$1.5~$^{\circ}$C for the Fluke and $\pm$2~$^{\circ}$C for the FLIR, with no current Fluke calibration certificate. These limits exceed the order of the 1~$^{\circ}$C target and are not a combined uncertainty budget for the installed system. Emissivity and viewed area affect infrared readings; contact and mounting affect the PT100. Complementary characterization must use a calibrated contact reference, separate adjustment from verification, compare the three nodes at multiple temperatures and account for reference, conversion, lead, mounting and repeatability contributions. Absolute accuracy and inter-node repeatability remain unestablished.

\textbf{Field observation (case study).} The suction-fan motor history includes rises and falls and a recorded maximum of 70.0~$^{\circ}$C (Fig.~\ref{fig:serie}). Plant observations associated rising segments with periods when the machine stopped while the fan continued spinning unloaded; no synchronized machine-state log establishes the timing or lag of each transition. The thermal image then provides spatial context for the hotspot (Fig.~\ref{fig:termica}). Subsequent inspection associated the case with a bearing anomaly. This sequence---thermal history, operational context, thermal image and later inspection association---illustrates the use of historical data in condition monitoring. It does not establish diagnosis, prediction, lead time, causality, sensitivity or false-alarm performance; load, ventilation, mounting and other factors were not controlled.

\textbf{Instrumented activity and temperature range.} A firmware-instrumented interval was 2.279~s in eight of ten documented starts, with small variations reported for the remaining two. Instrumentation begins after initial serial setup and delay, so this is not total physical power-on-to-service time or synchronized network latency. Dividing by the 120~s cycle gives approximately 1.9\% instrumented duty cycle; complete reset recovery remains to be measured through reception and persistence at the gateway. Across the individual historical files, surface temperature spanned 15.3--70.0~$^{\circ}$C, with per-node means of 39.5--45.4~$^{\circ}$C. These statistics describe recorded thermal behaviour, not calibrated range or measurement uncertainty.

\textbf{Estimated battery autonomy.} A controlled five-day partial discharge under the nominal 120~s operating cycle used a 1000~mAh cell from 13 to 18 August 2026. Two voltage readings, 4.112~V and 4.089~V over 120~h, give a slope of approximately 0.19~mV/h. The generator \texttt{analisis/autonomia\_bateria.py} extrapolates that slope to 3.30~V for a conservative linear estimate of 177 days. Its assumed typical LiPo open-circuit-voltage/state-of-charge curve, fitted to the same two points, gives 291--307 days at cutoffs of 3.30 and 3.00~V, respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:autonomia}). This range expresses two cutoffs under one model, not a statistical confidence interval. The curve is a project modeling assumption without a documented primary cell characterization.

Both values are estimated autonomy, not experimentally demonstrated lifetime. The linear result is conservative relative to the LiPo model but is not a guaranteed physical lower bound. Temperature, load, relaxation, ADC resolution, aging and self-discharge can alter the projection from this small voltage drop. Model-implied average current is approximately 0.13--0.24~mA; neither it nor the assumed microcontroller sleep current is an integrated measurement of the complete node. The 1.9\% instrumented duty cycle does not establish energy consumption. A full discharge to a specified cutoff and current logging by operating state are needed; no automatic doubling is claimed for the 2000~mAh BOM cell.

\textbf{Capabilities and limitations.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
\item Acquires periodic surface-temperature readings with a three-wire PT100 and configurable per-node offset; probe class, rated assembly range and full part identification remain undocumented.
\item Raw availability is 76.0\%, 32.3\% and 75.1\% in the individual windows; conditioned link availability is 97.8--98.4\% and PDR is inferred at 95.3--96.6\%, using different gap filters.
\item Operates locally with a rolling 30-day per-sensor store; older dates are removed after rotation. Pre-rotation exports preserved the records analysed beyond 30 days, including Node B's longer history. LittleFS is an alternative storage medium, not a mirrored backup. Direct web CSV export is unverified and primary CSVs are absent from the reviewed repository.
\item Five pairs support field agreement after adjustment to the same Fluke reference. The project-reported 0.4~$^{\circ}$C maximum is not reconstructible from those pairs. Absolute accuracy requires an independent calibrated contact reference and an uncertainty budget.
\item A five-day partial discharge supports estimated autonomy of 177 days or 291--307 days under the stated model and cutoff assumptions; extended lifetime and complete-node current consumption were not experimentally demonstrated.
\item LoRa range of at least $\approx$688~m in a non-line-of-sight urban path, with the link operating down to $\approx-98$~dBm RSSI (test stopped voluntarily, not by link failure).
\item The exact E220 variant and final pilot transmit power are not verified. The interface used did not expose SNR, and synchronized end-to-end latency and complete service recovery were not measured. Three deployed nodes do not constitute controlled inter-node repeatability or a network-capacity test.
\end{itemize}
